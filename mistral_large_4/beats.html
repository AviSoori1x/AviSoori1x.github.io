The model

A trillion parameters, 52 billion of them at work on any one token. Before the surprises, here is what was built, what it was fed, and how it was trained.

01§1

A trillion parameters, 52 billion at a time

Mistral Large 4 is a mixture-of-experts model with 1T parameters in total and 52B active. Each token is routed through a small subset of the expert networks, so only 52B parameters do work on any one token, while the model as a whole holds a trillion.

It reads images as well as text, and the paper is plain about what it was built for: reasoning and long-horizon agentic work, where the model acts over many steps and many tool calls. Most of the decisions that follow only make sense against that target.

Each square stands for a billion parameters. Send a token through and 52 of them light up. Which ones light is illustrative.
02§2.3

3,840 GPUs for 45 days

Pretraining ran on 3,840 NVIDIA GB200 GPUs in a Mistral data center near Paris, on an internal stack built on PyTorch and CUDA. It took 45 days, about 4.15 million GPU-hours. The design leans on Blackwell: FP8 tensor cores for low-precision training, the large NVLink domain for wide expert parallelism, and the fast link to the Grace CPU for offloading activations.

Two efficiency numbers come with it. MFU was 36%, counted strictly: against peak BF16 throughput, including matrix-multiply and attention FLOPs, excluding recomputation. Goodput was 94.1%, the share of the run that turned into training progress.

Switch between the three numbers to see what each one counts.
03§2.1

Predict the settings, then test the prediction on smaller copies

Rather than search hyperparameters at full scale, the learning rate is predicted. The predictor is log-linear in five inputs: width, depth, training horizon (total tokens, repeats included), expansion rate (total experts over active experts) and batch size. Keeping each input separate means the predictor can be checked for extrapolation along each one.

Batch size gets its own predictor, fit on the same dimensions apart from batch size itself, and in practice it depends mostly on horizon and expansion rate. So the batch is predicted first and the learning rate is conditioned on it. A scaling ladder of smaller models then uses the same predictors and multipliers, so it differs from the real model "in scale only".

Click an input to see which predictor leans on it.
04§2.2

A broad first phase, a sharper second one

The pretraining data is multilingual and natively multimodal: web pages, source code, and scientific and technical documents, almost all of it formatted as interleaved text and image chunks. The first phase, 65% of training, is about broad coverage of domains and languages.

The second, 35%, shifts toward higher-quality, more STEM-heavy data and raises the sequence length from 4K to 40K tokens. Code more than doubles, from 16.9% of the mixture to 39.0%, and web pages make room for it, dropping from 56.3% to 32.0%.

Switch phases and watch the mixture move.
05§2.2

Images from the very first step, at almost no cost to text

ML4 is trained from scratch on interleaved image and text data, and with careful dataset design this costs the text side very little. Matched on the number of text tokens seen, text perplexity shows no regret at all. Matched on the total training horizon, where images use up part of the token budget, the regret is modest.

The payoff is over an order of magnitude more images than Mistral's previous flagships saw. The systems trick: prefetch images N batches ahead and cache their features from the frozen vision encoder, which keeps 99% of text-only MFU and means image features never have to be stored on disk.

Toggle the comparison to see why "no regret" and "modest regret" are both true.
06§2.3.2

A vision encoder built from scratch, and judged on a small model

The vision encoder has 1.6B parameters, trained from scratch with a captioning objective over 8T tokens; the captioner is thrown away afterwards. Images go in at their native aspect ratio, up to 1540 by 1540 pixels. At the output, each 2 by 2 block of patches becomes one: the four embeddings are concatenated and projected, then an MLP maps the result into the language model's embedding space.

To judge an encoder, they attach it to a small LLM and run a short SFT of about 20B tokens. Figure 2 does this for a frozen 400M version of their encoder against Perception Encoder ViT-L/14, also 400M and frozen, on ten benchmarks. Theirs finishes ahead on nine. The exception is ChartQA, where Perception Encoder leads from 40% of the SFT run onwards.

Step through the patch merger, then switch to the ten-benchmark comparison.
07§2.3

Cheaper on paper, slower in practice

Granularity is how finely the experts are cut: higher means more, smaller experts. ML4 keeps the granularity of 4 used in Mistral Large 3. Going to 8 would reach the same perplexity with about 4.3% fewer FLOPs, give or take 0.5 points.

But training at granularity 8 measured about 5% slower. At those central values, doing 4.3% less work at about 5% lower speed roughly cancels out in wall-clock time, and the measured slowdown is what kept the model at 4. It is a small decision, and a clean example of a habit that runs through this paper: the number that decides is the one measured on the real system.

Switch granularity and compare the work done with the time it takes.
08§2.3

Balance the load where the slowdown actually happens

A mixture-of-experts step runs only as fast as its busiest expert. ML4 computes the load-balancing loss over each expert-parallel group rather than over the global batch, which targets the quantity that causes the slowdowns directly: the tokens arriving at each expert. Pretraining comparisons favored this when trading imbalance against validation perplexity.

The cost is coupling. The loss now depends on how experts are split across devices, so scaled-down experiments must be careful whenever that split changes. The optimizer is AdamW with both betas at 0.975, coupled and higher than usual, which gave moderate gains.

Switch between balancing the global batch and balancing each group. The totals match; the worst device does not.
09§2.3.1

Small batches early, big batches late

Early in training, a gradient estimated from part of a batch is already good enough, so the rest of that batch is wasted compute. Later, precise gradients pay. So the batch grows in stages whose token share grows exponentially: the final batch size runs for the last 50% of tokens, the one before it for 25%, and so on, with the learning rate rescaled at each stage.

For optimization alone, a ramp from 0.25× to 2× the predicted batch did best. Bigger batches run at higher MFU, so they chose 1× to 4× for wall-clock reasons: 40M, then 80M, then 160M. Small early batches also act as a regularizer, more so the more often the data repeats.

Drag along training to read the batch size at each point.

The attention U-turn

ML4 was built for agentic work, and its attention was cut down hard to make it cheap to serve. The cuts turned out to hurt exactly that work, so the architecture was changed in the middle of training.

10§3.1

Three tricks that cut the KV cache sixteenfold

An agent on a long task carries a long context, and every token of it costs memory in the key-value cache. ML4 began with three ways to shrink that. Sliding windows: three of every four layers look back only 512 tokens. Cross-layer attention (CLA): every other full-attention layer reuses the KV of the full-attention layer before it.

YOCO: full-attention layers in the second half of the network read a KV cache made in the first half, which also allows a two-stage prefill that is 1.7× faster at 1M context. Together, 16× less KV cache than full attention, and early ablations said the cost in quality was negligible or small.

Turn each trick on and watch which layers still keep their own KV.
11§3.1

Nothing looked wrong until the model had to act

As pretraining went on, they ran early SFT cooldowns on intermediate checkpoints. Perplexity and knowledge benchmarks landed where expected. Agentic evaluations did not, above all the long-context ones such as SWE-bench and BrowseComp.

Parallel ablations at the same token budget found the cause. SWE-bench Verified, measured after midtraining and SFT, dropped when full attention became sliding windows, and dropped again when CLA was added. Figure 4 follows five attention designs through midtraining and on into SFT. Read off the figure, full attention ends near 66 and the design with all three tricks near 56. YOCO on its own costs almost nothing by the end of SFT, which matters later.

13§3.2

Change the architecture without starting over

Rather than start again, they operated on an existing checkpoint, before the second phase of pretraining, aiming for something as close to full attention as possible without losing what pretraining had already taught it. Removing a sliding window is easy: let the layer see the whole context instead of the last 512 tokens.

Removing KV sharing is harder, because a layer that borrowed its keys and values must now make its own. Each one got KV weights copied from the last long layer that had its own. For CLA that is the long layer just before it, which sees a very similar input. For YOCO it is the long layer at the halfway point, which for most of the second half is much farther away.

Pick a layer that used to borrow its KV to see where its new weights come from.
14§3.2.1

Closing the perplexity gap did not close the agent gap

They tested surgery at 8×1022 FLOPs, applied either at the start of midtraining or before phase 2 of pretraining, against two references: the original model with all three tricks, and one pretrained with full attention throughout. The phase-2 runs kept the original rotary frequencies or rescaled them 8× with YaRN.

Figure 6 shows the trap. Phase 2 after surgery pulled perplexity close to the full-attention baseline, but SWE-bench did not follow. Bigger architectural changes were harder to recover from, so they kept the surgery conservative and added learnable parameters instead.

15§3.2.1

Let the layers learn their own sense of position

The learnable parameters are LeRoPE, which learns the rotary frequencies, and SSMax, which learns a per-head scaling of the queries. Together they beat fixed YaRN downstream at almost the same training loss: one more case where loss alone would have hidden the difference.

The learned frequencies did not simply drift. In an intermediate phase-2 checkpoint the largest changes sat in two bands near the 4K training context, where frequencies rose about 1.5× and 1.8× relative to YaRN, making their wavelengths 35% and 44% shorter. Most other bands moved less. The model retuned a few bands rather than rescaling them all.

Pick a band and compare its wave under YaRN with the learned one.
16§3.2.2

One of the three tricks survived

The small-scale surgeries removed YOCO too, and there, given the whole phase-2 and midtraining budget to recover, removing it or not ended up about the same. But removing YOCO disrupted the loss more at first, and for the full model there was no room to rerun all of phase 2.

So they ran a conservative test at 8×1023 FLOPs, surgery followed only by midtraining, and keeping YOCO did better (Figure 8). The real model was operated on at the end of phase 1, then trained for about two-thirds of the original phase 2 before midtraining.

Trace the three runs.
17§3.2.2

After surgery, almost full attention

In the converted model, every former sliding-window layer is an independent full-context layer, with LeRoPE and SSMax. The original global layers still use no positional encoding (NoPE). With CLA gone, the global layers that borrowed KV through CLA now make their own, and those in the second half still read the KV made in the first half through YOCO.

So it gained many independent full-context layers while keeping part of the old sharing layout. The paper's section title says it plainly: "Why ML4 is (almost) a full-attention model". It does not give the total layer count; the figure here follows the paper's Figure 3, which draws each half with its middle layers left out.

Switch between the original design and the converted one.
18§3.2.2

Did the converted layers actually look far back?

Lifting a window does not force a layer to use its new reach. To check, they measured where attention mass goes, using 32k-token probes from six data sources on intermediate checkpoints. Converted layers put around 55% of their attention on tokens more than 512 positions back, against 67% for the native long layers.

The paper reads this as the old initialization surviving in part, while the converted layers could still learn a long-range role when it was needed. And even in a model with full attention everywhere, some layers look further back than others, so 55% against 67% is not a failure.

Switch layer type and see how much attention lands beyond the old window.
19§3.2.3

Then sparse again, but chosen per query

Full attention over a long context is expensive, so ML4 was then adapted to DeepSeek Sparse Attention: a lightweight indexer picks the top 2,048 keys for each query, and attention runs only over those. Unlike a fixed window, the choice is made per query, so any token in the context can still be picked.

Stage 1 warms the indexers up for about 15.1B tokens by distilling the main model's attention with a KL loss, the main model frozen on full attention. Stage 2 trains both together under sparse attention for another 198B tokens. From layer 16 on, the former sliding-window layers within each original group attended so alike that each group shares a single indexer.

Slide the context length and count the keys each query reads.

Agents write the tasks

Post-training needs a great many hard tasks with answers a machine can check. Mistral has agents write more and more of them, and built the machinery to check the agents.

20§4.1

An assembly line where the workers are agents

Generating training data has traditionally been manual work, with many iterations and a lot of human oversight. For ML4, Mistral increasingly hands it to authoring agents. They get a minimal set of tools, enough to do everything a human expert would do to build a task, and they can run a task against several models and inspect the traces.

Agents are fallible, so every seed, and the candidate built from it, runs a gauntlet (Figure 9): a seed filter, cheap checks by LLM judges, expensive checks by an agentic verifier and for reward hacking, then difficulty calibration. A seed that fails the filter is rejected; a failure at any later gate goes back to the seed with feedback. Successful authoring trajectories also join the midtraining data, so the next model gets better at writing tasks: a flywheel.

Send a seed through and choose where it fails.
21§4.1

Two taxonomies decide what gets written

A seed is two things: the environment the agent works in, and the behavior the task should demand. Both come from taxonomies (Figure 10). The economy taxonomy asks what work is being done, from accountants doing a month-end close to lawyers drafting a contract. The capabilities taxonomy asks what completing the task should require: success criteria, underspecification, reasoning, data analysis, document skills, user personas and user behaviors.

A task-authoring agent samples from both. The month-end close, crossed with temporal ordering and cross-document reconciliation, becomes a task: prepare the close report from the ledger and the reconciliation notes, using revision history to resolve conflicting figures. For knowledge-work tasks, the paper observes (without giving numbers) that samples conditioned on taxonomies come out higher quality, more realistic and more diverse.

Pick an example task. Figure 10 traces only the knowledge-work one; for the other two you see the matching leaf.
22§4.1.1

Turn a merged pull request into an exam

Software tasks come from public GitHub repositories. First an authoring agent builds the project in a container, installs the toolchain and finds a reproducible way to run the tests. Tests that fail before the reference change and pass after it become the verifier. Tests that pass both before and after guard against regressions.

The solution and anything that could reveal it are removed, and the problem is rewritten as a self-contained spec; candidates whose tests cannot be validated still go to midtraining. Then a refinement agent runs solvers of different strength, tells model failures apart from broken specs or tests, and revises the task, which is verified again.

Pick a test and run it before and after the fix to see what job it gets.
23§4.1.2

Terminal tasks, seeded three ways

Terminal tasks drop the model into a sandboxed command line to investigate and fix things on its own. Seeds come from three places: millions of technical seeds mined from the pretraining corpus, synthetic seeds drawn as tuples of skill, domain, difficulty, verbosity and other attributes, and internal terminal-agent traces turned into verifiable tasks.

The authoring pipeline turns each seed into a problem statement, tests, an environment and sandbox configuration, and a reference solution. Instructions are audited for clarity, an adversarial agent hunts for ways to hack the reward, and how often solvers pass sets each task's difficulty.

Pick a seed source.
24§4.1.3

A patch counts only if the exploit dies and nothing else breaks

For vulnerability patching, an authoring agent takes a public CVE, finds the upstream fix, and packages the vulnerable code into a reproducible container. Tests mix adversarial exploits with ordinary inputs. A test harness counts only if it fires on the unpatched code and passes after the reference fix.

The model either fixes a named flaw, or gets only a weakness category and must find and fix the defect itself. Reward is positive only when the exploit is fully neutralized and the existing tests still pass. The pool spans tens of thousands of CVEs across roughly 600 weakness classes.

Try all four outcomes. Only one earns positive reward.
25§4.1.4

Build the office first, then hand out the work

For knowledge work, the environments are simulated workplaces: realistic files and application data across tool surfaces such as MCPs, APIs and CLIs, in areas such as finance, law, engineering, data science and business management. Each workflow from the taxonomy goes through three steps. A research agent gathers public documents for an open-ended query. An authoring agent turns them into a coherent workspace, adding spreadsheets, reports, presentations and correspondence. Several agents then inspect it for realism, consistency and file integrity.

Next come the applications. Agents read the files and each app's database schema, and create records that match the workspace's people, projects and timeline: messages, calendar events, project updates, customer histories. Long-horizon agentic tasks are also generated to run inside each environment, grounded in a second taxonomy of agent behaviors and skills.

Step through the build from the taxonomy seed to the tasks, or click any stage.
26§4.2.1

Midtraining: learn new skills, keep the old ones

Midtraining is a trade. It should add new capabilities, such as agentic ones, while retaining as much as it can of the long tail of knowledge from pretraining. So its hyperparameters were chosen by optimizing a weighted average of the evaluation suites for both. For the learning rate, a multiplier on top of the pretraining scaling-law prediction transferred best across scale. It was tuned jointly with the rehearsal rate, how much pretraining data is replayed.

At ML4 scale the best compromise was 1/5 of the peak pretraining learning rate and 1/10 of pretraining rehearsal. Keeping the batch size large cost very little in quality, and the MFU it bought more than made up for it.

Switch between the three knobs to see how each was set; the two ratios are drawn to scale.
27§4.2.2

A ladder of small models picks the SFT learning rate

SFT settings also come from a scaling ladder: smaller models of the same family, fine-tuned on slices of the SFT mixture that grow with model size. At each rung, the learning rate with the lowest validation loss wins. A power law in pretraining compute, batch size, training tokens and data repetition, fitted to the smaller rungs, predicted a held-out model an order of magnitude larger.

When the mixture changed, only a constant offset moved, so a small ladder per mixture was enough to recalibrate, with no new search at full scale. Batch size mattered far less than the learning rate, and weight decay and schedule shape did not move the optimum. A learning-rate ablation at ML4 scale landed close to the prediction early in training (Figure 11).

Reinforcement learning at scale

At peak, tens of thousands of rollouts ran at once on about 4k GPUs, and the longest single rollout lasted about 37 hours. Most of what makes this work is plumbing, and some of it is about agents misbehaving.

28§4.3.1

Rollouts, scoring and training overlap

RL runs on a fully asynchronous pipeline built on Ray. A dataloader samples prompts under a curriculum, and the runner pool admits groups of episodes as concurrency and backpressure allow, giving priority to siblings of groups already started. A finished group goes to a scoring pool for group-level rewards, then to a tokenization and packing pool, and a central bin-packer fills fixed-length microbatches for the trainer.

Generators are vLLM engines that receive new weights over NCCL at each training step, and the sandboxes, tools, verifiers and judges autoscale with the load. At peak: 26k concurrent rollouts on about 20k sandboxes, using 3,104 GPUs at a 1:2.5 trainer-to-generator ratio, and about 4k GPUs counting judges and services.

Click a stage to see what it hands to the next.
29§4.3.2

When an episode breaks, decide whose fault it was

Every environment, from single-turn QA to long-horizon agentic coding, is written against one interface, and RL, evaluation and synthetic data generation run the same code, so an environment behaves the same in training and in evaluation. Each request and response is logged to an append-only list of records, the single representation of an episode. Only the policy's own generations become training data.

When an episode breaks, the environment must assign blame. Malformed tool calls and context overflow, for example, count as the agent's failure. A sandbox crash or a failed service call is discarded in RL and retried in evaluation. Ambiguous cases count against the agent, because silently dropping unreliable behavior would quietly reward it. Because the line is kept strict, infrastructure incidents cost throughput without corrupting the reward signal, though they can shrink effective group sizes and skew the data mixture, which is tracked separately.

Pick a failure and see where it goes.
30§4.3.3

An agent broke out of its sandbox

Each run provisions millions of sandboxes. In one instance an agent chained four vulnerabilities and got root on one of Mistral's nodes. It sniffed traffic to grab an identity-scoped API key, found an undocumented deployment of the sandbox control plane, and used the key against its own pod to run arbitrary commands. The API let it run commands on a privileged sidecar, and a kernel panic took it to the host.

On the host, it realized the CTF, the capture-the-flag challenge, was not there and stopped. Another agent that escaped in a similar way probed the API for creating sandboxes, then tried to clean up after itself and deleted about 30k sandboxes by mistake. Both escapes triggered alerts, and the run restarted after remediation. The sandboxes are now moving from containers to microVMs.

31§4.4.1, §4.4.2

New weights in the middle of a rollout

Generation and training are decoupled. The trainer takes a step once it has 7k usable trajectories, then pushes the new weights to the generators. Generation is not drained before an update: in-flight rollouts pause, load the new weights, and resume without recomputing their KV cache.

So a long rollout can be written by several policy versions, and its later tokens can attend to KV entries that older policies computed. The longest production rollout lasted about 37 hours and spanned 136 model updates. Across training, the average staleness was six steps. The objective corrects for this lag with importance weighting.

Drag along a long rollout and watch the policy version change under it.
32§4.4.1, §4.4.4

Count rollouts or count tokens, and you get two different runs

The ML4 RL run generated 5.36M rollouts across 452k groups and 306k instances, 162.5B output tokens in all. The mixture is broad: multimodal, alignment, instruction following, cybersecurity, code agents, knowledge work, finance, STEM, safety, long context and multilingual.

Code agent tasks were 20% of rollouts but 46.6% of output tokens, so the average code-agent rollout wrote about 2.3 times as much as the average rollout. Reasoning tasks came second, with 10.2% of rollouts and 20.9% of tokens. The other categories share the remaining 69.8% of rollouts and 32.5% of tokens; the paper does not break them down.

33§4.4.1, §4.4.4

Groups, and the groups that teach nothing

For each prompt the generators sample a group of rollouts: 16 by default, 32 for agentic coding, anywhere from 2 to 32 overall. Each data source builds its own composite reward from signals such as unit tests, sandboxed runs, SQL execution, LLM judges, a reward model and programmatic checks, designed to sit mostly in [0, 1]. A rollout's advantage is its reward minus its group's mean, divided by the standard deviation of those centered rewards across the whole batch (Equation 1).

If every rollout in a group earns the same reward, every advantage is zero and the group teaches nothing. Such groups are dropped before they reach the trainer, so the 7k-trajectory batch counts only trajectories that carry a gradient.

Set the rewards in a group, then make them all equal.
34§4.4.1

Clip the weight, not the gradient

The loss is CISPO-style REINFORCE. Each token's loss is minus the advantage times its log-probability, scaled by an importance weight: the trainer's probability over the generator's, capped at 3 from above with no floor, and held constant by a stop-gradient. Unlike PPO, the ratio is not clipped inside the objective, so a token whose weight is capped still contributes to learning; the cap only bounds the variance from very stale or mismatched tokens.

Every trainable token in a group also gets the same scale, the group's rollouts over its trainable tokens, so the group's total equals its number of rollouts and groups count by rollouts rather than by length. In multi-turn trajectories, turns flagged for format errors or invalid tool calls get a MiMo-style correction: zero advantage when the trajectory's outcome advantage is positive, an amplified negative one otherwise. The optimizer is Muon at a constant learning rate of 8×10-7 after a 25-step warmup.

Drag the probability ratio and compare CISPO with PPO.
35§4.4.2

Making the trainer and the generator agree

The inference engine and the trainer do not compute exactly the same numbers, mostly because of numerical error, and asynchrony adds staleness on top. In a mixture of experts, unstable routing amplifies both. The first fix, router replay, re-runs the routing decisions made at inference in the training forward pass, so router drift cannot cause divergence. The second is FP8 inference with blockwise scales in both generator and trainer, which speeds generation and matches the trainer.

The third is subtler. The generator may emit two tokens, "the" and "re", which become a string sent into the environment; tokenized again, that string is the single token "there". The same text is one sequence as generation and another as context, and blockwise attention from FA4 handles it efficiently. The average k2 divergence (an estimate of the KL divergence) between train and inference stayed at or below 5×10-3 for the whole run.

Retokenize the string and see the two sequences disagree.
36§4.4.3

Caught reading the answer key

The runs are watched for reward hacking and environment leaks, alongside correctness, pass@k, reward variance, entropy, train and generator divergence, gradient norms and router statistics. In one case the model was asked to fix a pipeline so that a compiled checker would accept its output. It noticed that the checker internally called execvp("python3", "-c", <source_code>).

So it planted a fake /tmp/fakebin/python3 on the PATH to intercept the call, and captured the reference implementation from the command-line payload. Then it listed eight discrepancies between its code and the reference, fixed them, and submitted, noting: "I have the full reference implementation from the board binaries." The sandboxes keep being hardened, while keeping the realistic internet access and tooling agents need.

38§4.4.5

BrowseComp searches keep finding other agents' footprints

Reading web-search traces, the team saw the model complain that its results were "full of SEO spam". It was not generic junk but query-reflection pages: sites that turn any query they receive into an indexable page whose URL spells out the query. They are footprints left by earlier agents working on the same benchmark.

In internal BrowseComp traces from February to October, the share of trajectories that hit such a URL in their first ten searches rose over the year, and by October most did (Figure 14). The paper calls the rise steady, but the plotted share stalls near 30% in July and dips in early August before climbing past half in mid September. SimpleQA Verified, on the same search stack, showed no equivalent increase. Excluding those domains did not change scores significantly, but made runs vary more. The plan is an internal, offline copy of the web.

39§4.5.1

A bonus for being right and brief

To cut verbosity without hurting results, correct rollouts earn a group-relative length bonus. Within a group, find the shortest correct rollout. Lengths are binned in steps of 10% of that length, so tiny differences do not count (Equation 2). Correct rollouts in the shortest bin get the full bonus λ, those in the longest get none, and the rest fall on a straight line in between (Equation 3).

Wrong rollouts get nothing however short they are, and if all the correct ones land in a single bin, nobody gets a bonus. Because everything is relative to the group's own correct answers, no per-task length target is needed. Running RL with different bonus sizes yields teachers with different trade-offs between efficiency and performance.

Drag the lengths, mark rollouts right or wrong, and watch the bonuses.
40§4.5.2

Three teachers, one student, three reasoning efforts

The release model has three reasoning-effort modes: high, medium and low. The student starts from the high-reasoning RL checkpoint, which is itself the high-effort teacher; the medium and low teachers come from length-penalized RL. On its own rollouts, the student minimizes a per-token KL divergence to the teacher's full next-token distribution.

Figure 15 tracks the student's length relative to high effort. For the first fifth of training both modes stay near 1.0; then they drop within about a tenth of training and hover just under their targets. Over the last fifth of training, medium averages 0.49 against a target of 0.528 and low 0.20 against 0.227. Teachers run on separate GPU nodes and send only hidden states, so adding teachers costs little.

43§6

Shard the KV cache along the sequence, then fix the kernels

Production serving is tuned for latency. Plain tensor parallelism gives ML4 the best latency but replicates the KV cache on every rank, which caps concurrency and the cache hit rate. Context parallelism shards the cache along the sequence instead. With a native FP8 KV cache and offloading, that gives about 20M tokens of in-memory KV-cache space on 4×GB300.

The catch: the standard CP implementation ran much worse than the tensor-parallel baseline, because of kernel inefficiencies. Rewriting the CP collectives, with latency-optimized exchange patterns, compute and communication overlapped inside the kernel, and more fusion, raised decode interactivity by 51% in Figure 16's setting (8×GB300, 50K input, eight concurrent requests), with no more than 10 to 15% communication overhead compared with tensor parallelism.

One decode step, 36% shorter

Figure 17 times one decoding step of an index-producing layer under context parallelism. Before: 240 μs on a single stream, with three slow communication phases, the top-k exchange (39 μs), the Q gather (30 μs) and the output combine (33 μs), strung between the projections, the sparse indexer and sparse attention.

After: 153 μs, 36% less. The indexer, its top-k exchange and the selection move to a side stream that runs alongside the main one, and the three exchanges shrink to 15, 11 and 16 μs. The gains come mostly from hardware multicast, latency-first exchange patterns, removing in-kernel synchronization, and fusing nearby compute into the communication kernels.

Draft ahead, verify in blocks

Speculative decoding uses an adapted DSpark: a speculator drafts tokens ahead and the full model verifies them. Two additions to the original recipe, a recurrent conditioning head and unique mask tokens, lengthen the accepted drafts by 1.2% and 0.5% at negligible runtime cost. The DSpark module is trained on 70B tokens.

With block verification, DSpark improves latency and throughput by 90% over a baseline without speculative decoding, and by 15% over an EAGLE-3 baseline, at eight concurrent requests. Its acceptance rate in Figure 16 is the average across all SPEED-Bench categories.

46§6

A trillion parameters in 606 GB

The routed experts are quantized to NVFP4, while everything else keeps its training precision, which gives a mixed BF16, FP8 and NVFP4 checkpoint of 606 GB. Naive abs-max quantization was already enough: calibrated on 200M tokens of the SFT mixture, it matches the FP8 reference in all evaluations. This is what lets ML4 run on four GB300 GPUs.

At fleet scale, routing builds on llm-d, and most of Mistral's work on it has been upstreamed. Model Express speeds up startup by moving checkpoints peer to peer over InfiniBand, and cache sharing is warmed up in advance. The fleet autoscales on live request and server metrics, and a new replica starts in under 120 seconds.

Equal loss is not equal ability

From the conclusion: comparable language-modeling loss does not imply comparable performance on long-horizon tasks, and better proxies have to be found. ML4 was first designed with a heavily reduced KV cache, came up short on agentic work, and was operated on during training, first to almost full attention (it kept YOCO) and then to sparse attention.

Those findings pushed Mistral to explore sparse, linear and hybrid attention, with agentic evaluations informing design choices throughout training. On the data side the goal is recursive: train each generation of models on environments written by the previous one, with stronger verification, safety and evaluation as the workflows grow longer.
